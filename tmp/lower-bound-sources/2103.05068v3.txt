                               Cutoﬀ for the Asymmetric Riﬄe Shuﬄe

                                                                   Mark Sellke∗




                                                                      Abstract
        In the Gilbert-Shannon-Reeds shuﬄe, a deck ofN cards is cut into two approximately equal parts which
        are riﬄed together uniformly at random. Bayer and Diaconis [BD92] famously showed that this Markov
        chain undergoes cutoﬀ in total variation after  3log(N)2log(2)  shuﬄes. We establish cutoﬀ for the more general
        asymmetric riﬄe shuﬄes in which one cuts the deck into diﬀerently sized parts. The value of the cutoﬀ
        point conﬁrms a conjecture of [Lal00].  Some appealing consequences are that asymmetry always slows
        mixing and that total variation mixing is strictly faster than separation andL∞ mixing.

2   Preliminaries

Let Pp denote the probability measure on the symmetric group SN given by applying a p-shuﬄe to the
identity.   Given two discrete probability vectors p = (p0,...,pk−1) and q = (q0,...,qℓ−1) deﬁne their
convolution
                                 p∗q≡ (p0q0,p0q1,...,p0qℓ−1,p1q0,...,pk−1qℓ−1).
This convolution turns out to correspond to shuﬄe composition.

Proposition 2.1 ([DFP92, Example 7]).  Performing a q-shuﬄe followed by a p-shuﬄe is equivalent to
performing a (p∗q)-shuﬄe. That is,
                                                     Pp∗Pq =Pp∗q.

    Proposition 2.1 yields an explicit description for the distribution Pp∗K of a deck after K shuﬄes.  For
instance in the “symmetric” setting of [BD92], it implies that composing a k1-shuﬄe and a k2-shuﬄe results
in a k1k2-shuﬄe. It will actually be more convenient for us to work with the inverse permutations. We now
explain how to do this, following [Lal00]. First deﬁne a distribution on sequences

                                                      S = (s1,...,sN)

of length K strings as follows. Generate N strings of length K, all with i.i.d. p-random digits in

                                                   [k]0 ={0,...,k−1}.

in which i,i + 1∈ V(G) are neighbors if and only if si = si+1, and no other edges are in G.  Hence G
is a union of disjoint paths, which we call G-components.  (We say S and G = G(S) are p-random when
they are constructed in this way.)  Finally choose a uniformly random permutation π∈SN and deﬁne its
G-modiﬁcation πG by, within each G-component, sorting the values π(i) into increasing order.  The next
proposition states that πG is exactly the inverse permutation of a p∗K-shuﬄed deck.
Proposition 2.2 ([Lal00, Lemma 3]).  Let π∈SN  be uniformly random and G = G(S) be p-random as
deﬁned above, for some ﬁxed positive integers N and K.  Then the distribution of (πG)−1 is exactly Pp∗K.
In particular, the total variation distance of πG from uniform equals dN(K).

    In other words, the inverse permutation of a shuﬄed deck can be generated by starting with a uniformly
random permutation π, and then modifying π to create πG which is increasing on an independently random
set of subintervals in [N].  After more and more shuﬄes, these subintervals shrink in distribution, leading
eventually to mixing. In fact, L∞ and separation mixing both correspond to G having no edges with high
probability, see [Lal00, Corollary 3] and [ADS12]. However because G is random, total variation mixing can
anddoesoccursooner. Wereferthereaderto[Lal00,Section2]formoreexplanationandexamplesregarding
Proposition 2.2. In brief, the N sequences si∈ [k]K0  correspond to the sequences of pile-types that each of
the N cards in the deck appears in during the shuﬄes. The sorting within G-components corresponds to the
fact that if two cards are in the same pile during all K of the riﬄe shuﬄes, then their relative order must
be preserved.
    Throughout the remainder of this paper, we work entirely with this transformed problem. Namely we
will show that for K≥ (1+ε)CplogN the permutation πG has total variation distance o(1) from uniform,
while for K≤ (1−ε)CplogN this distance is 1−o(1).
